The New Urban Planning Standard or Eco-Gentrification? A Report on Land Use, Livability and Equity in Barcelona's Superblocks

Intro

The Barcelona grid network was originally designed in 1855 by renowned urban planner Ildefons Cerdà to combat issues such as overcrowding, poor ventilation, and disease outbreaks, all of which were plaguing the city (Amati et al., 2023; Castrezzati, 2023). In the 21st century, the new plague has become cars and motorized traffic, with 85% of the roads historically being dedicated to vehicles (Amati et al., 2023). As highlighted by the New York City Department of City Planning (2024), urban design needs to go beyond aesthetics and "enhance people's daily lives" by allowing people to walk comfortably whilst "confronting society's greatest challenges", which in Barcelona’s case entails improving traffic, air quality, and walkability. Barcelona’s Superblocks (Superilles) model does this by restructuring 3x3 grid cells into 10 km/h loops, reallocating over 70% of space to pedestrians (Amati et al., 2023; Anguelovski et al., 2023; Iturralde, 2021). This report aims to evaluate the Superblocks initiative by analysing its land-use and spatial efficiency, analysing the quality-of-life (QoL) split between the internal streets and the perimeter corridors, as well as assessing how environmental progress leads to property speculation and green gentrification (Anguelovski et al., 2023).

Section 1

Reallocating street space from motor vehicles to pedestrians has been one of the primary spatial successes of the Barcelona Superblocks model, aligning directly with core urban design principles that prioritize accessibility and safety as well as mobility and connectivity (New York City Department of City Planning [NYC DCP], 2024; Rueda, 2019). Historically, Barcelona’s roads and public spaces were overwhelmingly dedicated to motorized vehicles, at 85% and 60% respectively (Amati et al., 2024). This imbalance severely restricted street walkability and health-supportive open space across the city (Amati et al., 2024; Castrezzati, 2023). The Superblock design model targets allocating no less than 70% of public space to pedestrians, framing streetscapes as primary, multi-use public realms in contrast to relying on isolated Modernist parklands (Amati et al., 2024; Rueda, 2018). The restructuring of 3x3 grid cells into internal 10 km/h circulation loops forces vehicles to turn and exit back to the perimeter roads instead of driving straight through (Amati et al., 2024; Iturralde, 2021). This design eliminates through-traffic while preserving essential access for residential, delivery, and emergency services (Amati et al., 2024; Anguelovski et al., 2023; Iturralde, 2021). By pushing non-local vehicles onto surrounding perimeter corridors, former car-dominated roads are transformed into green, walkable public spaces that foster human connection, active transit, and public health (Amati et al., 2024; Anguelovski et al., 2023; Pérez et al., 2025). Ultimately, while reallocating street space achieves spatial efficiency that fulfills key principles of urban design, redirecting vehicular traffic into perimeter corridors calls into question the socioeconomic and distributional quality-of-life impacts on the residents living along those exterior streets (Anguelovski et al., 2023).
