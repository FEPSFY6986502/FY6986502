Abstract

Introduction

The Barcelona grid network was designed in 1855 by urban planner Ildefons Cerdà to combat issues such as overcrowding, poor ventilation, and disease outbreaks, all of which were plaguing the city (Amati et al., 2023; Castrezzati, 2023). In the 21st century, the new plague has become motorized traffic, with 85% of the roads being dedicated to vehicles (Amati et al., 2023). As highlighted by the New York City Department of City Planning [NYC DCP] (2024), urban design needs to go beyond aesthetics and "enhance people's daily lives" by allowing people to walk comfortably whilst "confronting society's greatest challenges", which in Barcelona’s case entails improving traffic, air quality, and walkability. Barcelona’s Superblocks (Superilles) model does this by restructuring 3x3 grid cells into 10 km/h loops, reallocating over 70% of space to pedestrians (Amati et al., 2023; Anguelovski et al., 2023; Iturralde, 2021). This report evaluates the Superblocks initiative by analysing its land-use and spatial efficiency, analysing the quality-of-life (QoL) split between the internal streets and the perimeter corridors, as well as assessing how environmental progress leads to property speculation and green gentrification (Anguelovski et al., 2023).

Analysis and Discussion

Reallocating street space from motor vehicles to pedestrians is a primary success of the Barcelona Superblocks model, aligning directly with urban design principles that prioritise accessibility, safety, mobility, and connectivity ([NYC DCP], 2024; Rueda, 2019). Historically, Barcelona’s roads and public spaces were dedicated to motorized vehicles, at 85% and 60% respectively (Amati et al., 2024). This imbalance restricted street walkability and open space across the city (Amati et al., 2024; Castrezzati, 2023). The Superblock design model targets giving no less than 70% of public space to pedestrians, framing streetscapes as primary, multi-use public realms in contrast to relying on isolated Modernist parklands (Amati et al., 2024; Rueda, 2018). The restructuring of 3x3 grid cells into internal 10 km/h circulation loops forces vehicles to exit back to the perimeter roads (Amati et al., 2024; Iturralde, 2021). This design cuts through-traffic while preserving essential access for residential, delivery, and emergency services (Amati et al., 2024; Anguelovski et al., 2023; Iturralde, 2021). By pushing non-local vehicles onto surrounding perimeter corridors, former car-dominated roads are transformed into green, walkable public spaces that foster human connection, active transit, and public health (Amati et al., 2024; Anguelovski et al., 2023; Pérez et al., 2025. However, redirecting traffic into perimeter corridors calls into question the socioeconomic and quality-of-life impacts on residents living along exterior streets (Anguelovski et al., 2023).

When redesigning a city, urban planners must consider the locals and how changes affect their daily lives, and the superblocks model is no exception. While the model succeeds in meeting its promises of lower emissions and noise pollution, this comes at the expense of a disparity in the QoL of the residents. While people in the “winner” districts (inside superblocks) can enjoy the improvements listed above, this is not the norm for residents living closer to outside streets (“loser districts”), who still experience the noise and air pollution of the old city model. For example, after traffic calming in 2017, Viladomat Street (an arterial corridor in the exterior of the San Antoni Superblock) saw a 20% increase in traffic volume (~10,300 vehicles) by 2019 (Anguelovski et al., 2023). Residents living in these arteries reported a heightened feeling of traffic accident risks and noise exposure. Pressing upon this further, focus groups and qualitative research in neighborhoods such as Poblenou and Horta revealed that residents felt that traffic, air pollution, and noise had simply shifted into surrounding areas and not fully disappeared. We can confirm this from the 15% decrease in traffic in the internal streets, in contrast to the minimal 0.9% decrease in traffic in the streets in the wider vicinity, highlighting the lack of meaningful change in certain areas (Anguelovski et al.,2023). In terms of air quality, while the residents living in the “winner” districts enjoyed the 25% reduction in NO2 and 17% drop in PM10, (data from Sant Antoni’s central square), “loser” corridors still experienced conditions close to the original baseline, with only 10% of the residents feeling a noticeable decrease in air pollution, underscoring the disparity between the two areas of the superblock (Pérez et al.,2025). The disparities between the two districts highlight inner districts as “secluded islands of environmental privilege” (Anguelovski et al.,2023), which leads to green gentrification and the displacement of local people from their homes due to property speculation.

The environmental street calming interventions and the addition of green areas transform superblocks into desirable urban areas; however, this desirability is the reason for property speculation due to green gentrification (Anguelovski et al., 2023) (Curran and Hamilton 2017, Gould, and Lewis 2017). Green gentrification is the idea that urban greening agendas shift the demographic of the area, leading to increased house prices and the displacement of working-class people from these areas (Anguelovski et al.,2023). This agenda centers Barcelona as a climate leader, making it an attractive city for international firms, international real estate investment, and expat workers, accelerating the green gentrification (Cocola-Gant and Lopez-Gay 2020). Residents that were surveyed in the Superblocks, especially the ones with school-aged families and housing-rights civic groups (i.e. Observatori dels Barris de Poblenou; La Flor de Maig), fear displacement due to the increase in urban greening, making a link between displacement and the attractiveness of the Superblock model. Although local citizens welcome the improved environmental changes, there is a looming fear that they could be displaced. To combat the eco-gentrification, the municipality of Barcelona has put in place anti-displacement measures, such as enforcing a 30% social housing quota for new residential builds, with Poblenou benefitting from 18 protected housing units within the superblock and 2,300 units across the broader Sant Marti district. This shows that physical street conversions and aggressive housing protections need to go hand in hand for green urban transformations to help civilians.

Barcelona’s Superblocks show that reclaiming street space enhances urban liveability, but achieving true socio-spatial equity requires pairing environmental transformations with aggressive housing protections as well as network-wide traffic management.
